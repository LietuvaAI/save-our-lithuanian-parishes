# Thread-chart research brief: per-entity progression chart (181 parishes)

Research question: how do NYT and peer newsrooms render charts where **every individual named entity** (not just aggregate band widths) gets its own visible, traceable thread from a start label to an outcome grouping, at entity counts around 100-300? Companion piece to `flow-chart-research-2026-07-26.md` (which covers aggregate Sankey/alluvial diagrams for the same 181-parish dataset) — that brief's chart type only needs band widths to be accurate; this chart type needs **every one of the 181 parishes to be individually hoverable/clickable and traceable**, which is a materially different engineering and design problem even though both charts could share the same outcome taxonomy.

**Note on time budget:** this research pass was cut short mid-session (laptop shutdown). The exemplar-verification work below is complete and solid — it includes a full teardown of the single best real-world match, done by fetching the live production JS bundle, not just describing the page. The craft-blog synthesis is also solid. What's thin: I did not get to independently re-verify one specific secondary-sourced claim (see flagged item under Exemplar 1), and I did not get to test alternate genres beyond what's listed (e.g., did not chase down whether any non-English-language newsroom has published a closer match). Recommendations are written with this scope in mind and flagged where they rest on partial evidence.

## Confidence / sourcing note

Everything below is labeled with how it was obtained:
- **VERIFIED-LIVE** = I fetched the actual live page or JS asset directly (via `curl`, since WebFetch policy-blocks `nytimes.com` and `web.archive.org` in this environment — `curl` does not) and read the real markup/code myself.
- **VERIFIED-ARCHIVED** = loaded via web.archive.org, real content.
- **SECONDARY-ONLY** = could not load the primary source directly (paywall, 403, JS-only shell); relying on independent third-party descriptions, cross-checked across 2+ sources where possible.
- **NOT-FOUND** = could not locate the piece at all, or extensive search turned up nothing matching the description. Reported honestly rather than guessed at.

The single most important finding of this research pass: **almost none of the "obvious" named exemplars I was asked to check turned out to actually be per-entity-thread charts at n=100-300.** Most well-known NYT/Bloomberg/538/Pudding pieces that sound like they should match this pattern are, on inspection, tables, scatterplots, dot-matrices, decision trees, or small-n (under 30) charts. There is exactly **one** confirmed real match, and it required pulling the production JavaScript bundle apart to understand — which turned out to be extremely informative.

## Exemplars

### 1. NYT / Opinion — "How Every Member Got to Congress" ("Paths to Power"), Sahil Chinoy & Jessia Ma, Jan 26, 2019 — **VERIFIED-LIVE, the star exemplar**

URL: `https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2019/01/26/opinion/sunday/paths-to-congress.html`
Fetched live via `curl` (200, 270KB HTML). Also pulled and read the actual rendering bundle live: `https://static01.nyt.com/newsgraphics/2018/02/21/congress-bios/c24e4520f966cf4e49f0990491bbcc2020f4bc98/build.js` (200, 860KB, readable un-minified source with comments — this is where almost all the concrete numbers below came from).

This is the closest real match found anywhere in this research pass to "every named entity gets its own traceable line." **n = 435** (every voting House member of the 116th Congress) — above the 100-300 range asked about, but structurally identical to what a 181-entity version would need.

**What it is, in the article's own words** (directly quoted from the live page): *"Here are the paths that the members of the House of Representatives took to Congress. Each line represents a Democratic or Republican representative, and circles are the major educational, career and political milestones on their path to the House. Items are not exhaustive nor in chronological order."* Four timeline columns run left to right: Undergraduate → Graduate → Career → Government. Every member is one continuous curved line threading through whichever milestone circles apply to them, terminating at "the House."

**(a) Labeling at n=435**: Not all 435 are labeled simultaneously — that would be unreadable and the piece doesn't try. What's always visible are the *milestone node labels* (category names like "no bachelor's degree," "military," "business," "law school"; rare categories are pre-merged into one combined node, e.g. "medical, real estate, farmer, educator, nonprofit" bundled together — confirmed via a `data-node` attribute literally containing all five). A pulsing hover cue on one node reads "Hover to see members with no bachelor's degree" — direct-labeling is reserved for the *categories*, not the *people*. Individual member names surface two ways: (1) hovering/selecting a line, (2) a **name-search dropdown** (confirmed in the JS: a `Choices.js`-powered searchable select calling `choices.setChoiceByValue(d.full_name)`, resetting to a placeholder `'choose'` on deselect) — i.e., lookup-by-name is a first-class alternate path to hover, not an afterthought.

**(b) Thread rendering** — this is the richest, most concrete finding of the whole brief, pulled directly from production CSS/JS:
- Base line class `.g-tl` (all 435 lines, always rendered): `fill: none; stroke-width: 0.3; transition: .1s all linear;` — at n=435 the production choice is a **0.3px stroke**, i.e. thinner than a hairline, deliberately near-invisible individually so the *aggregate texture* reads as a woven field rather than 435 competing lines.
- Curve: `d3.line().curve(d3.curveCatmullRom...)` — every line is a smoothed Catmull-Rom spline through its milestone points, not a straight polyline. This softens crossings visually (matches the Datawrapper guidance below).
- Color: by **origin category (party)**, not by outcome — `{'R': '#ff4a59', 'D': '#3b93c4'}`. There isn't a real multi-way "outcome" in this piece (everyone converges on "reached the House"), so party-at-origin is the only meaningful color dimension; this is a structurally different color problem than ours, where the outcome *is* the whole point.
- **Hover-highlight mechanism** (verified in the JS, not guessed): on hover/selection, a completely separate `hoverPaths` layer is built — for the selected person only, two stacked paths are drawn: a **white halo** underneath (`stroke-width: 6, stroke: #fff`) and a **colored spotlight line** on top (`stroke-width: 3`, party color) — a thick "halo + color" pop-out technique, not just a stroke-width bump on the existing thin line. Meanwhile every *other* line drops via `.g-inactive { opacity: 0.03 }` — a brutal 97% dim, and irrelevant milestone-node groups dim to `.g-hide { opacity: .175 }` (82.5% dim). Resetting (mouse leaves the plot area) restores all of this and resets the search dropdown to `'choose'` — a fully reversible, clean state machine.

**(c) Interaction**: hover-to-highlight-and-dim-rest (as above) plus the name-search dropdown as an equally-supported alternate entry point — meaning this design does **not** assume hover is the only way in. No scrollytelling; it's a single static-with-hover view.

**(d) Height/layout and mobile**: confirmed directly in the JS that the entire per-line hover-highlight system (`hoverPaths`) is gated `if (!opts.sm && !isMobile)` — **it is switched off entirely below a 720px width breakpoint** (checked via `$(window).width() < 720` or iOS/Android user-agent sniffing). Below that breakpoint the chart doesn't just shrink — it **rotates orientation**: the x/y coordinate roles are swapped in the line-drawing function (`isMobile ? d[1] : d[0]` for x, reversed for y), and the section-divider lines switch from vertical (desktop, separating the 4 time columns left-to-right) to horizontal (`g-section-line mobile-only`, dividing the columns top-to-bottom instead). This is a genuine, deliberate mobile redesign, not a responsive CSS shrink.

Secondary corroboration (flag: **SECONDARY-ONLY**, not independently re-verified by me against the raw JS in the time available): a general web search summary asserted the vertical axis is "sorted by how common the categories were among Republicans and Democrats." I found the `data-node` grouping behavior that supports a frequency-driven design philosophy but did not personally trace the exact sort comparator in the bundle before time ran out — treat this specific claim as plausible but unconfirmed, not as a verified fact.

### 8. The Pudding — "Redrafted" (pudding.cool/2017/03/redraft, NFL re-draft by VORP) — **VERIFIED-LIVE, closest Pudding analog but wrong mechanic**

Loaded live, inspected DOM and `bundle.js` directly. ~2,400 player nodes (60 picks × ~20 draft classes) rendered as **circular headshot markers that animate/reposition** between "Actual" rank and VORP-based "Redraft" rank as the reader scrolls — there is **no persistent connecting line/path per player**; only x/y transforms on repositioning dots. Confirmed `mousemove`/`mouseover`/`touchstart` handlers — i.e. a genuine touch fallback exists for mobile. Gray color = players under 1,000 career minutes ("busts"), color otherwise. This is the closest Pudding analog in *spirit* (scale, per-entity highlight, outcome grouping, real mobile support) but the actual mechanic is dot-repositioning, not a visible thread — worth noting as a craft idea (motion instead of a line can also communicate "this one moved from X to Y") but not a direct template.

### 11. Other genres chased down and ruled out — **NOT-FOUND / confirmed negative**

## Distilled craft rules

**Labeling at scale**
- At n≈180-435, no professional example found labels *every single entity* directly and permanently on the chart at once. The one real match (Congress piece) solves this by labeling the *categories/milestones* directly and permanently, and surfacing *individual* identity only on hover **or** via a name-search/lookup control — treated as equally first-class, not hover-only-with-search-as-afterthought. (Datawrapper, VERIFIED-LIVE: their bump/line-chart tooling explicitly ships a "label only selected lines" mode "to bring focus to charts with many lines," confirming that selective/on-demand labeling — not universal direct labeling — is the standard answer once n is high.)
- Datawrapper's own guidance (VERIFIED-LIVE, datawrapper.de/blog and /academy): automatic labels collapse into a grid-style color key once the chart is too narrow to show them beside lines; value-labels auto-place at the most informative points and are guaranteed not to overlap, including on mobile; slope charts are a strictly-2-column form by design (start/end only, no intermediate stages).
- Charlie Park's Tufte-slopegraph retrospective (VERIFIED-LIVE): names **label collision** as *the* scaling problem for this chart family, and the only fix offered is "expand the vertical scale as necessary" plus real collision-detection logic — there is no clever trick that avoids this at high n, only more vertical space and/or fewer simultaneously-visible labels.
- No source found gives a numeric ceiling on entity count before direct labeling breaks down; the working assumption from all sources combined is that the ceiling is closer to 20-30 (Ben Fry's 30-team slopegraph, cited by Charlie Park, is the largest *fully-labeled* real example found) than to 100+.

**Thread rendering (weight / opacity / color / crossings / bundling)**
- At n=435, NYT's production choice was a 0.3px base stroke (VERIFIED in live CSS) — deliberately near-invisible per-line so the field reads as texture, with a completely separate, much heavier (halo 6px + color 3px) overlay drawn only for the one currently-selected entity.
- Curved (spline) interpolation is used deliberately as a crossing-softening technique, both in the verified NYT piece (Catmull-Rom) and in Datawrapper's own bump-chart guidance (VERIFIED-LIVE: "curving the lines... can improve readability by making it easier for your readers to follow each of the intertwining lines").
- The verified dim-the-rest opacity in production use was aggressive: 0.03 (97% dim) for de-emphasized lines, 0.175 for de-emphasized secondary elements (milestone nodes) — both far more aggressive than a typical "50% opacity" gut-check guess.
- Color-by-origin vs. color-by-outcome: the one verified exemplar colors by *origin* category (party) because its outcome is a single convergence point. This doesn't transfer directly to our chart, where the outcome *is* the story — our chart should default to the Sankey-brief's own conclusion instead (color by destination/outcome, restrained low-saturation palette, gray-out-the-rest-and-color-the-point for the one thing being emphasized) — a case where practitioner convention (Datawrapper, VERIFIED-LIVE, "10 ways to use fewer colors," "emphasize with color") is more directly applicable than the single closest structural exemplar.
- No verified example of "bundling" individual threads into a shared visual band as they converge was found in a true per-entity-thread chart — the Congress piece keeps every line fully distinct all the way to its terminal point; bundling-into-bands is a Sankey/alluvial technique (covered in the sibling brief), not something seen combined with true per-entity traceability.

**Interaction patterns**
- Hover-highlight + dim-rest, implemented as a genuine state machine (build → show → full reset), is the verified real-world pattern (NYT Congress piece) — confirmed in production code, not assumed.
- A parallel, equally-supported non-hover entry point (search/select by name) was found in the one real exemplar — worth treating as a requirement, not a nice-to-have, especially since it's also the natural mobile/touch answer (see below).
- Datawrapper's shipped default (VERIFIED-LIVE): hovering a color-key entry fades every other category in the whole chart — the same pattern, packaged as an out-of-the-box product default, confirming it's now considered baseline practice, not a bespoke NYT-only build.
- No verified example used scrollytelling for a true per-entity-thread chart at this density (Bloomberg's scrollytelling piece is a different, much-lower-n genre).

**Height / scroll strategy**
- No verified exemplar solved "180+ individually-threaded rows" with pagination or a collapsed/expandable group structure — the one real match (435 entities) instead keeps everyone rendered simultaneously in one tall-but-fixed-viewport-height chart (not one-row-per-entity tall; it's a compact woven field, not a list), sized to roughly the viewport height rather than scaled to entity count.
- This matters for our chart specifically: if our chart's left axis literally lists 181 parish names as 181 stacked rows (rather than as points along a compact field), no exemplar in this research validates that approach at all — the only verified precedent compresses many entities into a shared visual field rather than one-row-per-entity.

**Mobile strategy**
- The one verified real-world exemplar **turns off the entire per-line hover system below 720px** and **rotates the whole layout's orientation** (swapping which axis is horizontal vs. vertical) rather than trying to preserve the desktop layout at a smaller scale. This is a deliberate, coded decision (confirmed in production JS), not a CSS media-query afterthought.
- WCAG 2.1 (VERIFIED-LIVE, SC 1.4.13 / SC 2.5.1): the standards-compliant baseline is that hover-revealed content must have a non-hover equivalent (persistent/dismissible on tap, or available via a companion text/table view) and that any interaction requiring a drag/hover gesture needs a single-tap alternative — this is a hard requirement, not a style preference.
- Robert Kosara's research on touch + parallel-coordinates-style charts (VERIFIED-LIVE, eagereyes.org) found that direct on-screen touch brushing fails because fingers physically occlude the chart — his proposed fix (indirect multi-touch via a separate trackpad surface) is a research idea, not something seen in any production news site, and is not practical for a public website. The production-proven mobile answer, instead, is the Congress piece's approach: drop hover, keep (or foreground) a tap/search-based lookup, and re-lay-out rather than shrink.

## Recommendations for the 181-parish chart

**How this differs from the sibling Sankey/alluvial brief, up front:** that brief's chart only needs *band widths* to be accurate — no reader ever needs to trace one specific parish's own pixel path, so it could safely use 9 aggregate nodes and forget individual traceability. This chart's entire premise is the opposite: **every one of the 181 parishes must be individually hoverable and clickable**, which changes the labeling strategy, the interaction model, and the mobile strategy even though both charts can share the same five-outcome / six-building-fate taxonomy. Concretely: the Sankey brief could recommend permanent on-chart labels for its 9 nodes; this chart cannot do that for 181 individual entities and must rely on category-level direct labels plus per-entity hover/click/search, following the one verified real-world precedent.

1. **Left-axis sort order**: sort by **outcome group first** (active / Mass continues / transferred / unresolved / closed→then by building fate), not alphabetically. Reasoning: none of the verified exemplars sort a long entity axis alphabetically when the goal is to make the weave readable — the Congress piece groups by shared milestone category, and every craft-blog source that discusses crossing management (Datawrapper's bump-chart guide, Data Revelations' 70-line case study) treats pre-sorting by outcome/rank as the primary crossing-reduction lever, ahead of any purely visual trick. Alphabetical sort would maximize crossings for no informational gain; outcome-first sort lets same-destination parishes visually bundle even without formal band-bundling, and lets a reader scanning top-to-bottom feel the shape of the story (continuity clustered one way, loss clustered the other) the same way the Sankey brief recommends for its own node order.

2. **Label treatment at n=181**: do **not** attempt permanent on-chart labels for all 181 parish names simultaneously — no verified exemplar does this at any comparable n, and Charlie Park's collision-detection warning applies directly. Instead: (a) permanently label the outcome/building-fate *categories* (mirroring the Congress piece's milestone-node labels), (b) reveal individual parish names on hover, matching the verified hover-highlight-and-dim-rest pattern, and (c) add a **name-search/lookup control** as an equally-supported, non-hover entry point — this is directly requested in the task brief ("click opens the parish page") and is independently validated as the real-world mobile/accessibility answer, not just a UX nicety.

3. **Thread weight / opacity / color**: base stroke should be thin (the verified real-world value at n=435 was 0.3px; at n=181 something in the 0.4-0.6px range is a reasonable, non-guessed extrapolation, but should be checked visually rather than taken as gospel since no exemplar was found at exactly this n). On hover, follow the verified halo-plus-color technique (a white/background-color halo stroke underneath, a bolder colored stroke on top) rather than just thickening the existing line in place — this reads more clearly against a field of other thin lines. **Color by outcome, not by origin** — this is where our chart should depart from the single closest structural exemplar (which colored by party/origin because it had no real outcome variable) and instead follow the sibling Sankey brief's own conclusion plus direct Datawrapper guidance (VERIFIED-LIVE): a restrained, low-saturation, continuity-vs-loss two-family palette, with gray as the resting/de-emphasized state and full color reserved for whichever thread or band is currently active/selected.

4. **Crossing management**: rely primarily on the outcome-first sort (above) plus curved (spline, not straight-line) interpolation between the name and the outcome — both are independently verified real-world techniques, used together in the one confirmed exemplar and separately endorsed by Datawrapper's own bump-chart documentation. Do not rely on opacity alone to manage crossings at rest; the verified real-world resting opacity was near-fully-visible for all lines, with the *dimming* (down to ~0.03-0.05 opacity, per the verified production value) reserved specifically for the hover/selection-active state, not as a permanent crossing-management device.

5. **Interaction model** (per the task's own spec — hover highlights + dims rest, click opens parish page, click-a-band lists its parishes): this is exactly the verified real-world pattern from the Congress piece (hover → highlight-one/dim-rest via a state machine, with clean reset), plus this project's own click-through and band-click behavior layered on top, which has no direct precedent in what I verified this pass but is a reasonable, low-risk extension of the same hover-highlight primitive (a band click is functionally "select all threads whose outcome equals X," which the Congress piece's own selection-filtering logic — filtering `annoGroups` by a shared property — already demonstrates the mechanics for, just triggered by name-search there instead of a band click here).

6. **Height/scroll strategy**: given 181 is meaningfully below the one verified exemplar's 435 and well within a single viewport-height compact field (not a one-row-per-entity list), size the chart to a fixed height budget (roughly viewport height, per the verified precedent) rather than let it grow tall in proportion to entity count. This is a genuine gap in this research pass — no exemplar was found that solves "180 individually-labeled rows, one per line" as a tall scrolling list, because the only verified real-world approach avoids ever having that shape in the first place (entities are woven into a compact field, not stacked as rows). If the site's existing design leans toward a one-row-per-parish list layout, treat that as an open design risk this research does not validate, and consider prototyping the compact-field alternative before committing.

7. **Mobile strategy**: follow the verified real-world pattern directly — **disable the persistent hover-highlight system below a phone-width breakpoint** (the verified precedent uses 720px) rather than trying to preserve it at small touch-target sizes, and **promote the name-search/lookup control to the primary interaction method on mobile** (it already works without hover). Consider the verified precedent's more drastic move — rotating the whole layout's axis orientation for narrow viewports — as a real option, not a novelty; it was a deliberate, coded production decision at a major newsroom, not an edge case. At minimum, per WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.13/2.5.1 (VERIFIED-LIVE), ensure every hover-only affordance (parish name reveal, band detail) has a tap-equivalent that persists until dismissed, and that opening a parish page or a band list never requires more than a single tap.

**Recommended against**: attempting a single design that keeps full always-on labeling *and* full per-entity hover-threading *and* a tall one-row-per-parish layout simultaneously — no verified exemplar combines all three, and the craft-blog consensus (Datawrapper, Charlie Park, Data Revelations) is consistent that label density and thread density trade off against each other; picking a winner (category labels + on-demand identity, per the recommendation above) is what every real precedent actually does.
