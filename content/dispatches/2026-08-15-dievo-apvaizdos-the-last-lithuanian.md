Dievo Apvaizdos — Divine Providence Lithuanian Catholic Church — is the last Lithuanian Catholic parish in the Detroit area, and one of six remaining in the United States. It stands in Southfield, at Beech Daly and Nine Mile Roads, with a cultural center beside the church that holds a Lithuanian school, choir, archive, dance groups, scout troops, ateitininkai, the Kovas sports club, the Lithuanian Daughters, and the Lithuanian Community. It is a personal parish under canon law, established by reason of language and nationality; 65% of its members live outside the parish boundary, driving in from across metropolitan Detroit, from Grand Rapids, and from Canada. The Archdiocese of Detroit is now deciding the future of all 209 of its parishes, this one among them.

#### A society before a parish

The parish did not begin with a bishop’s decree. It began with a mutual-aid society — the Lietuvių Šv. Jurgio draugija, the St. George Society, organized around 1894, meeting in a borrowed Polish hall on Canfield Street. When its members decided Detroit’s Lithuanians needed a parish of their own, they built it: a small wooden church at Westminster and Cardoni Streets in the spring of 1908, and the Šv. Jurgio — St. George — parish founded that year, the first Lithuanian parish in Detroit. The brick church followed in 1916–1917, the parish school in 1920. The school’s builder, Kan. Fabijonas Kemėšis, an editor of *Draugas*, later died in Soviet exile in Siberia in 1954; the news took two years to reach America. In the early 1920s the parish counted about a thousand adult parishioners, with three priests serving.

#### Displacement, twice

By the 1940s the old neighborhood was changing and Lithuanian families were leaving it. In 1948 the parish broke ground on Schaefer Road — and learned that the old name could not travel: the chancery rechristened the parish Divine Providence, then numbering about 187 families. When the new church was blessed in December 1949, Auxiliary Bishop Stephen Woznicki declared it “only for Lithuanians.”

Then came the freeways — twice. In February 1966 the original St. George church was demolished for the Chrysler Freeway; parishioners retrieved the cornerstone documents. Fourteen months later, in April 1967, the chancery notified the pastor that the Jeffries Freeway would take the Schaefer church as well. The community that had built itself a church in 1908 faced building a third.

#### In writing

The parishioners refused to accept just any parish. In May 1967 thirteen parish clubs signed the first letter to Archbishop John Dearden; in June a delegation met him and heard, “I do want you to have another church.” On August 18, 1967, the committee sent the curia its written demand: a Lithuanian parish, with no territorial boundaries. To prove the community was real, Dr. Adolfas Damušis ran a registration drive that signed up roughly a hundred younger families.

On December 2, 1968, some 260 parishioners met with the newly consecrated auxiliary bishop, Thomas J. Gumbleton, who favored an ordinary territorial parish. The parish’s anniversary book records the meeting candidly: the younger generation and the Knights of Lithuania argued the case; the committee chair, Antanas Dainius, called a vote; it was unanimous. Gumbleton closed the meeting with words the book preserves in Lithuanian — “Bus taip, kaip jūs norit” — it will be as you wish.

The letter arrived on December 31, 1968, from the Office of the Vicar for Parishes, written on behalf of Archbishop Dearden. It accepted the site at Beech Daly and Nine Mile and the parishioners’ decision: Divine Providence would be a national parish, where only Lithuanians register and the Lithuanians themselves provide the support.

#### The third building

A decade later the parish council still marveled at what the fight had cost: “Strange that, living in a free land, it took so much effort to win the right to build a national Lithuanian parish,” its 1978 book records. The community held up its side entirely. In February 1969 it paid the chancery $39,487.73 for the ten-acre lot at Beech Daly and Nine Mile, raised the construction money itself, and never touched the loan the Archdiocese had approved. The parishioners also insisted that a church and rectory alone could not preserve *lietuvybė* — Lithuanian identity — and the new Parish Council voted, nearly unanimously, to build a Cultural Center alongside; the Lithuanian press of the day wrote that “two houses are not a luxury but a necessity.”

Cardinal Dearden himself dedicated the church and Cultural Center over two days in September 1973 — the same year the Soviet regime was bulldozing the Hill of Crosses in occupied Lithuania. The church holds stained glass by Vytautas Kazimieras Jonynas — in a single window St. Casimir stands beside Grand Duke Vytautas and King Jogaila — and woodcarvings by Jurgis Daugvila. The Žiburys Lithuanian Saturday school moved in that year and is there still. Five years later the pastor, Fr. Viktoras Kriščiūnevičius, wrote in the parish’s anniversary book: “A good number of our parishes are fading as their surroundings change… Our parish, too, may one day face such trials.”

#### A new wave

When Lithuania regained its independence in 1990, after fifty years of Soviet rule, a new generation came to America. Divine Providence became, in the parish’s own words, “a beacon of hope” and a sense of “home” for the newcomers. They became cantors and lectors, joined the Bible-study and pilgrimage groups, and brought their children to the Saturday Lithuanian school — sixty children when the parish described itself to the Archbishop in 2020. During the pandemic, the parish’s live-streamed Sunday Masses were viewed by thousands each week.

The era had its grief. In July 2009 the parish’s administrator, Fr. Ričardas Repšys, drowned in Lake St. Clair trying to save a young man; the Archbishop of Kaunas came to Detroit for the funeral. Fr. Gintaras Jonikas arrived that September as the parish’s eleventh pastor.

#### The last one

Detroit once had three Lithuanian parishes, and through the 1950s and 1960s they acted as one bloc. Šv. Petro closed in 1995, on its pastor’s retirement; its contents were shipped to Lithuania. Šv. Antano survived a closure scare in 1989, when Cardinal Edmund Szoka chose to rebuild it after a fire; it was canonically closed on June 23, 2013, with 34 registered parishioners remaining, and merged into Divine Providence — its altar reliquary carried across the city, the result framed as “one Lithuanian Catholic community.” Both endings were diocesan decisions. A museum of lietuvybė, established in the parish’s rooms in 2015, keeps the memory of all three Detroit parishes. By the 2014 centennial the parish counted 265 families, 468 persons. Since 2013, Divine Providence has been the last Lithuanian Catholic parish in the Detroit area.

#### What is happening now

The parish has been through this before. When the Families of Parishes reorganization began, the parish wrote to Archbishop Vigneron in June 2020 asking that Divine Providence not be clustered with other parishes: Lithuania’s bishops supply it a priest, it runs at no financial burden to the Archdiocese, and its mission — serving Lithuanian Catholics in Lithuanian — is one no other parish can carry.

In March 2026 the Archdiocese published a restructuring workbook for each of its 209 parishes, and then three draft models for Divine Providence’s planning area. Two of the three would leave the parish without a regular weekend Mass. The parish’s own workbook shows over $1 million in unrestricted cash and effectively zero debt. The public-feedback window closed on July 31; final models are expected in April 2027, and implementation begins July 1, 2027. No decree merging or suppressing the parish, or relegating the church to profane use, has been issued.

Divine Providence is one of thirteen ethnic and national parishes still operating in the Archdiocese of Detroit, four of them in this planning area. Across the United States, six Lithuanian Catholic parishes remain: Divine Providence in Detroit, and parishes in Boston, Chicago, Philadelphia, Cleveland, and Los Angeles. Two Lithuanian Catholic missions serve Lemont and Mundelein, and Lithuanian Masses continue in six more communities — fourteen communities in all.

The record of dialogue so far is short. Archbishop Lionginas Virbalas, SJ, the Lithuanian Bishops’ Conference delegate for Lithuanian Catholics abroad, offered to provide a Lithuanian priest at the parish’s own expense; Archbishop Weisenburger declined the proposal. Lithuania’s Ambassador Gediminas Varvuolis sought a meeting during his June visit, and the Archdiocese declined to meet with him. Archbishop Virbalas visited in July and likewise was not granted a meeting. The parish has been without a resident pastor since Fr. Tomas Miliauskas, MIC, concluded his service in May 2026.

The parish’s life continues. Its registers from 2022 through 2026 record 12 baptisms, 20 First Communions, 32 Confirmations, and 5 marriages. This fall at least ten children are preparing for First Communion and Confirmation, and the re-formed youth choir will sing at Mass. The scouts have served Detroit’s Lithuanian youth for seventy-five years under the motto *Dievui, Tėvynei, Artimui* — for God, for Country, for Neighbor. The Šaltinis dancers have just returned from the North American Lithuanian Folk Dance Festival in Chicago, where more than 2,000 dancers gathered. The church itself is on Lithuania’s official list of cultural heritage significant to Lithuania in the United States, under the 2002 cultural-property agreement between the two countries.
